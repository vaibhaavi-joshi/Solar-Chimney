Simulation studies

Each study varies one thing and holds everything else fixed. All runs use the same energy-balance model, the same 3 × 3 m room and, unless the study says otherwise, the same hot day of 44 °C maximum and 31 °C minimum that meets the IMD heatwave threshold.

Read these together. Studies 1, 2 and 4 each look like a separate disappointment. Study 6 shows they are one effect: past roughly 25 air changes per hour the room stops responding to more airflow, so anything that only adds flow stops paying. That single mechanism set the final design.
STUDY 01

Does the phase change material extend useful airflow?

The original premise of the project. Identical chimneys, one with 20 kg of paraffin behind the absorber and one with none, run through a full 24 hours.

Airflow across the day

Rural home, metal sheet roof. If storage worked as intended, the green line would hold up after the blue one falls away. The brief drop to zero near dawn is the one moment the draft genuinely stops, when the cooled room and the outdoor air reach the same temperature.

Indoor temperature across the day

The same two runs, plus the unfitted room and outdoor air for reference. The two fitted lines sit on top of each other.

The storage works; the ventilation does not notice. Evening airflow rises from 468 to 497 m³/h, a gain of 6.2%. Indoor temperature is identical to one decimal place, and the draft lasts 5.1 hours after sunset either way. The absorber genuinely stays warmer, 37.4 °C at midnight against 32.2 °C without storage, so the paraffin is doing its job. The airflow it buys simply has nowhere useful to go.
STUDY 02

How much storage is worth carrying?

If 20 kg gives 6%, perhaps the mass was wrong. This sweeps 0 to 30 kg, then asks a harder question: is there any condition under which storage starts to matter?

Evening airflow against stored mass

Average flow over the four hours after sunset. Every extra kilogram is extra load on someone's roof.

Where storage should have mattered more

The gain from 20 kg of paraffin under five different conditions. The hypothesis was that a better-insulated room or a cooler night would let storage earn its place.

The hypothesis was wrong, in the opposite direction. Insulating the roof made storage matter less, 4.7% against 6.2%, not more. Across the whole 0 to 30 kg range the total change is under 4%, and nothing above about 15 kg is doing measurable work. This is reported as it came out rather than quietly dropped.
STUDY 03

What shape should the collector be?

Three sweeps: total area, the same area in different proportions, and the width of the air channel.

Collector area

Peak indoor temperature reduction against collector area, rural case. The curve flattens well before the panel gets large.

The same 3 m², six different shapes

Every point holds identical collector area and identical materials. Only the proportions change, and with them how far the unit rises above the roofline.

Air gap between glazing and absorber

Wider gaps keep improving in the model, but published smoke-visualisation experiments show reverse flow setting in once the gap exceeds roughly 0.3 of the chimney height. That boundary is marked, and the model has no term for it.

Shorter and wider wins, and that is not intuitive. Friction in the channel scales with length divided by hydraulic diameter, so widening the collector cuts resistance faster than lowering it gives up stack pressure. A 3.0 × 1.0 m panel stands 2.30 m above the roof and delivers 8.9 K. The same area as 1.5 × 2.0 m stands 1.15 m and delivers 10.2 K. Half the height, better performance. The first design was the tall one.
STUDY 04

What tilt angle is best for this latitude?

Standard practice is to tilt a collector to catch the most sun. Tilt also sets how much vertical height drives the stack effect, so the two pull in opposite directions.

Solar energy collected

Daily total on the tilted surface, Gurgaon, June.

Indoor temperature reduction

The same runs, rural case.

Losing three quarters of the sunlight costs 0.2 K. Going from 50° to 90° drops daily collection from 4.30 to 0.97 kWh/m², a 77% loss, while performance moves from 10.2 to 10.4 K. The two charts are deliberately separate: plotting them on one pair of axes would invent a relationship that is not there. 50° was kept because it balances rainwater shedding, wind loading and dust accumulation, not because it optimises anything thermal.
STUDY 05

Does it have to be designed per climate?

Six cities covering four of the five climate zones in the National Building Code, each at its own hottest month using IMD normals.

Temperature reduction by city

Rural figures are the peak of the day; urban figures are the overnight average, because that is when each housing type is in trouble.

The housing type matters far more than the city. Across a 42 °C Ahmedabad day and a 34 °C Bengaluru one, rural daytime relief only moves between 8.1 and 9.6 K. The split between a metal roof and a concrete roof is much larger than the split between climates. One caveat that belongs on the record: this model has no humidity term, so the Mumbai and Chennai temperatures are sound but their comfort implications are understated, since humid heat is more dangerous at the same reading.
STUDY 06

What is actually limiting performance?

Studies 1, 2 and 4 all failed in the same direction. This one tests why, by pushing airflow far past anything buildable and then asking how much of the airflow the solar collector is responsible for.

Temperature reduction against airflow

Collector area swept from 0.6 to 12 m², far past anything buildable. The curve bends hard: the table gives what each extra 100 m³/h of airflow buys in kelvin.

The solar collector against an identical unglazed duct

Same geometry, same openings, but a white absorber and no glazing, so almost no solar collection. Peak airflow under five conditions.

The building's own trapped heat is driving most of the draft. A 59 °C room beside 44 °C outdoor air is already a powerful chimney before any sunlight is collected. The glazing and black absorber add about 11% more airflow and, because flow is saturated, essentially no extra temperature reduction. Quadrupling the collector from 3 to 12 m² buys 2.8 K.
What this does not settle. The model has no reverse-flow term, and reverse flow is the main way an unglazed duct would fail. At night a bare metal duct radiates to the sky, the air inside becomes denser than the room air, and the draft can turn round and push outside air down into the house. Keeping the absorber above ambient is exactly what glazing and stored heat are for, and our own data hints at it: with storage the absorber sits at 37.4 °C at midnight, without it 32.2 °C, against roughly 34 °C outdoor air. Without storage the absorber has already fallen below ambient. Deciding this needs the physical rig, not the model.

What these studies changed

  • The collector was sized at 3.0 m² rather than 12 m², because saturation means the extra nine square metres buy 2.8 K
  • The unit became short and wide, 1.5 × 2.0 m, standing 1.15 m above the roof instead of 2.30 m
  • Storage dropped from the headline feature to an open question for the prototype to answer
  • Reflective roof coating was promoted, because once extraction saturates the only lever left is reducing the heat coming in
  • The prototype's job changed from confirming a number to testing whether glazing and storage prevent night-time reverse flow
Model limitations relevant to these studies. No reverse-flow term, so results at gap-to-height ratios above roughly 0.3 are outside the range published experiments support. No humidity term, so warm-humid cities are understated for comfort. The model overpredicts against one of its three validation studies.
