The problem

Only around 8% of Indian households own an air conditioner, and rural ownership sits near 1%. The barrier is not electricity access, since over 99% of homes are electrified, but affordability and grid capacity. Cooling already accounts for close to a quarter of India's peak electricity demand, and that power remains largely coal-fired.

The households left out face the worst heat inside their own homes. Metal and concrete roofs absorb heat all day and release it indoors long after sunset. Night-time indoor temperatures in Indian homes have been measured 2 to 5 °C hotter than outdoors, and Delhi has recorded a night minimum of 35.2 °C, leaving no overnight recovery.

What it does

Sunlight passes through glazing and heats a black absorber. The air in the channel behind it warms, becomes less dense, and rises. That buoyancy pulls hot indoor air out through the roof and draws replacement air in through a low vent. The entire driving force is under one pascal, so the design is as much about managing pressure losses as about collecting heat.

What it does not do. The chimney cannot cool air below outdoor temperature; it has no cooling mechanism. It removes the trapped heat that makes indoor conditions far worse than outdoor, which in these homes is a large effect.

Two housing types, opposite problems

Rural — metal sheet roof

Thin and light, so it dumps heat into the room fast during the day and cools quickly after dark. The device delivers roughly 10 K at midday and 2.5 K at night.

Urban informal — concrete roof

Heavy, so it stores heat all day and keeps radiating it inward after sunset. The same device gives about 3 K at midday and 7 K at night.
